Tuning a 1090 MHz ADS-B antenna

Every number here is read from retained NEC2dXS output. Free space, perfectly conducting wire, 0.5 mm radius, referenced to a real 50 Ω load. The controls step through the same configurations I ran. Nothing has been built or measured yet.

What the sweep shows

Resistance barely moves, 42 to 56 Ω across the whole 10 mm range. Reactance sweeps -3 to +51 Ω over the same span, close to 6 Ω per millimetre.

Trimming is therefore a reactance adjustment. The match improves because the resistance was already near 50 Ω before I started. At 64 mm the reactance sits at +2.83 Ω, close enough to zero that S11 reaches -22.28 dB.

The same gradient is a build instruction. Near the optimum, 1 mm off costs 2 to 3 dB, so cut long and trim in small steps.

What the angle actually does

The usual advice is that a groundplane sits near 36 Ω and drooping the radials to 45° brings it to 50 Ω. Resistance does climb as described, 25.7 to 62.9 Ω, passing through 50 Ω near 30°.

Reactance climbs with it though, +9.4 to +41.7 Ω, and the match depends on both. The best S11 in this fixed-length family is at 15° rather than 45°, and it only reaches -11.17 dB. Droop on its own does not produce a good match.

The radials carry return current and are part of the radiating structure. Tilting them gives that current a vertical component, so the geometry drifts toward a vertical dipole and R, X and resonance all move together. I set droop first for resistance and horizon response, then used length as a separate control for the reactance.

3 · Sweep the band

S11 against frequency, 950 to 1250 MHz. Toggle configurations to compare. The dotted vertical line marks 1090 MHz and the horizontal rule is the -10 dB target.

Resonance and best match

The selected candidate crosses X = 0 at 1083.8 MHz and reaches its deepest S11 at 1088 MHz. Resonance asks when reactance vanishes. Match asks when the impedance is closest to 50 Ω. They coincide only when R is exactly 50 Ω, so both are reported.

The 45° trace drops below -35 dB, but it does that near 1003 MHz, which is no use at 1090. A deep notch is only worth reading once you know where it sits.

4 · Gain against coverage

The three-element Yagi comparison, at 1090 MHz, in absolute dBi with the 50 Ω mismatch applied once. Both cuts are vertical co-polar realised gain.

Groundplane · 64 mm, 30° Yagi · 128 mm driven

Reading the two cuts together

Forward, the Yagi gives 8.09 dBi against the groundplane's 1.78 dBi, a difference of 6.31 dB in the same direction with each antenna's own mismatch included.

Azimuth is where it gives that back. A 97° half-power beamwidth and 16.6 dB front-to-back put everything outside the forward sector below the omnidirectional antenna, down to about -12 dBi off the back. A passive antenna adds no energy, so the forward gain is paid for in every other direction.

ADS-B traffic arrives on all bearings, so the groundplane is what I would build. The marker at 2.4° is where an airliner at 35,000 ft sits at 250 km. Useful range is won near the horizon rather than overhead.

What this does not establish

  • No hardware. No antenna has been built, and no VNA or SDR measurement exists. Every figure is simulated.
  • Idealised model. Perfectly conducting wire in free space. No connector, no coax, no common-mode current on the feedline, no mast, no roof, no surrounding buildings.
  • Approximated feed. A 6 mm voltage-source segment centred 3 mm above the radial junction is a finite numerical stand-in for a physical connector.
  • Open numerical diagnostics. The 0° and 45° baselines retain average-power-gain residuals of -5.45% and +9.07%, so their absolute gains carry less precision than the selected pair. I have not applied a correction to hide them.
  • A failed run is retained, not deleted. The original NBS-adapted Yagi seed failed its mesh-convergence gate and stays in the record as a diagnostic.
  • Third-party solver. The solver is the author-distributed NEC2dXS build, not a custom implementation.
