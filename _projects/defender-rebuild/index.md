## Scope

The Defender is fifteen years old and was still on its factory rubber. Before a long coastal trip I wanted the suspension tight, the servicing up to date and somewhere flat to sleep in the back. Everything was done on the driveway on axle stands over about three weeks, without a hydraulic press.

## The Threaded-Rod Press

Most of these bushes are a steel shell pressed into an eye. A workshop would use a 10-tonne press and the purpose-made sleeve kits run from $150 to $550. I made a puller from a metre of M16 high-tensile threaded rod, plain nuts and hardened washers for about $50. A deep socket acts as the receiver on one side, a smaller socket or washer stack pushes on the other, and winding the nut draws the bush through.

M16 was chosen because it passes through the bush sleeves. The thread is kept greased so the nut doesn't gall under load.

{% include image-gallery.html images="rod-press.jpg, press-rod-bench.jpg" height="500" caption="Figure 2: The puller on a rear lower link with an impact socket as the receiver (left). The rod and jam nuts on the bench (right)." %}

I also tried a cheap bush press kit bought online, with its own draw rod and thrust bearing. Partway through a panhard rod bush, with the tool through the eye and the nut wound up tight, the kit's rod snapped and cracked the bearing race with it. I went back to the high-tensile rod, which finished that bush and every one after it. For the same job I reused the steel shell cut out of a lower link as the pusher, which happened to be the right diameter.

{% include image-gallery.html images="cracked-washer.jpg" height="350" caption="Figure 3: Piece of the thrust bearing race that cracked when the rod snapped." %}

## Problems and Fixes

**Seized shells in the rear lower links.** The press pulled the rubber and inner sleeve out and left the outer shell rusted solid in the eye. I drilled out what rubber was left, cut through the shell from the inside with a hacksaw blade in several places and folded it in with a chisel. It took most of a day. I had started pricing replacement arms, but the only standard-height ones were in the UK. With a clean eye the poly bushes pushed in by hand.

{% include image-gallery.html images="lower-link-shell.jpg, lower-link-eye.jpg" height="450" caption="Figure 4: The outer shell after being cut and chiselled out, and the cleaned eye." %}

**Panhard rod bolt fouled a bracket.** With the nut off, the chassis-end bolt hit the radius arm bracket before it was halfway out. Lifting that chassis rail slightly on a jack, with the front tyres still on the ground, gave it enough room to come out. The second eye had weld on its face, so the receiver socket had nothing flat to sit against and the stack kept tipping. The two panhard bushes took hours and were the slowest part of the suspension work.

**Sleeves would not line up on refit.** With the rod off, the axle had moved sideways. A ratchet strap between the axle and chassis pulled it back until both bolts dropped through.

{% include image-gallery.html images="axle-strap.jpg, spring-strap.jpg" height="450" caption="Figure 5: Ratchet strap pulling the front axle across to line up the panhard bolts (left). Front spring tied off while the damper and turret were out (right)." %}

**Wrong thread pitch.** The factory M16 suspension bolts are fine pitch (1.5 mm). The Class 10 lock nuts I had ordered as equivalents were coarse. I only found out with the panhard rod in my hands. I refitted it with new M16 x 100 grade 10.9 coarse bolts I already had for the radius arms, which matched the nuts.

**Catalogue said one thing, the car another.** This chassis number sits just before a few mid-year part changes, so every part was checked against the chassis number and not the model year. That still missed one. The catalogue calls for a cartridge oil filter, but with the sump drained I found a spin-on can. The filter housing has been changed at some point. I read the number off the old can and bought the match locally.

{% include image-gallery.html images="oil-filter.jpg" height="350" caption="Figure 6: The spin-on oil filter that should have been a cartridge." %}

**Fuel filter priming.** This engine has no lift pump and I don't have the factory priming tool. I filled the new filter with clean diesel through the outer ports before fitting it and it started fine.

## Sourcing

Parts came from four suppliers across six orders, tracked in a spreadsheet by job, part number, torque and status.

- Several Land Rover fasteners are not stocked anywhere in Australia, mainly long M16 flange bolts and the shaped washers. I substituted grade 10.9 hex bolts with hardened structural washers under the head.
- Rubber was kept where the bush is meant to flex. Polyurethane went where the part pivots or was only a few dollars more: anti-roll bar mounts, panhard rod, A-frame and rear lower links.
- The A-frame ball joint was bought already pressed into its carrier. It cost more than a bare joint but needed no press.
- I ordered the front damper bushes to exact count and came up eight cup washers short for the second side, which held that corner up for a few days.
- The fastener supplier's price breaks meant 20 of something was often cheaper than 14. The leftovers were all used on later jobs.

## Sleeping Platform

The rear and middle seats came out and the tub was stripped to bare panel. There was white corrosion around the old seat bracket holes, which I cleaned back and primed. I modelled the frame in Onshape before buying timber.

{% include image-gallery.html images="frame-cad.webp" height="400" caption="Figure 7: Onshape model of the frame over the measured tub and wheel-box profile." %}

Two 90 x 45 rails sit on the wheel-box ledges and bolt down through the original seat bracket holes, so nothing new was drilled in the body. Four cross members span between them, with film-faced ply on top. Unused holes were blanked with bolts and rubber washers.

{% include image-gallery.html images="tub-stripped.jpg, frame.jpg" height="500" caption="Figure 8: Stripped tub with the seat bracket holes treated (left). The frame sitting on the wheel-box ledges (right)." %}

The full-width ply panels I had planned would not go in through the doors in one piece. I cut them in half, added a member down the middle to carry the new joint and ended up with five panels. The front one is hinged to reach the footwell underneath.

{% include image-gallery.html images="ply-fitting.jpg, ply-down.jpg" height="500" caption="Figure 9: Fitting the ply around the body profile, and the deck down." %}

### Insulation

Butyl deadener went on the roof and wheel arches, then 6 mm closed-cell foam, then stretch carpet glued over the top. The front and rear doors got butyl on the outer skins and a foam sheet behind the trim, replacing the torn factory liners.

{% include image-gallery.html images="roof-butyl.jpg, arch-butyl.jpg" height="500" caption="Figure 10: Butyl sheets on the roof and over a wheel arch." %}

The foam was the most frustrating part of the whole project. It is self-adhesive and I added spray contact adhesive to the metal for heat resistance. Once the two touch there is no adjusting it. The foam tears before the glue lets go, so a sheet that lands 10 mm out stays there. Where I tried to lift it, the surface ripped and left a rough finish. I packed those spots with offcuts to level them before carpeting over.

What worked was cutting and dry-fitting every piece first, sticking only the top edge, then peeling the backing downwards a little at a time. Joins were cut slightly oversize, pushed together and sealed with foil tape. I ran the foam under the frame rails so its edges are clamped and can't lift.

{% include image-gallery.html images="arch-foam.jpg, arch-carpet.jpg" height="450" caption="Figure 11: Foam on a wheel arch with taped joins, and the same arch carpeted." %}

The deck panels are carpeted separately. The carpet is stretched from the centre out and stapled on the underside, so any panel lifts out. Artificial turf covers the load floor underneath.

{% include image-gallery.html images="platform-carpet.jpg, hinged-panel.jpg" height="450" caption="Figure 12: Carpeted deck with storage below (left). The hinged front panel from the side door (right)." %}

## Lessons for Next Time

- Check the thread pitch on a bolt from the car before ordering nuts for it.
- Assume every bush shell is seized. Soak it the day before and have the hacksaw ready.
- Make receiver cups and a thick pressure plate for the press. Sockets and washers got me through, but slowly.
- Use rated high-tensile rod for a puller. The cheap kit's rod snapped where the plain M16 rod never looked like it.
- Order small hardware with spares. Exact-count orders stall a job over a two-dollar washer.
- Treat catalogue data as a starting point on an old vehicle and look at the part before buying.
- Measure the door opening before cutting ply to full width.
- Template the foam in cardboard and never peel the whole backing at once.
- Update the parts register as each job finishes. It was out of date within a week.
