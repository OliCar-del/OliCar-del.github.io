## Scope

My Defender is fifteen years old and was still riding on its factory rubber. Ahead of a long coastal trip I wanted the suspension tight, the servicing current and a flat bed in the back. I did the lot on the driveway, on axle stands, in about three weeks and without a hydraulic press.

I hit every nut and bolt with penetrating oil daily for a week before picking up a spanner, and every one of them came off easily. The bushes were the hard part.

## The Threaded-Rod Press

Most of these bushes are rubber bonded into a steel shell, and the shell is pressed hard into an eye on the arm. A workshop would shove them out on a 10-tonne press, and a purpose-made sleeve kit runs anywhere from $150 to $550. I built a puller instead: a metre of M16 high-tensile threaded rod, plain nuts and hardened washers, about $50 all up. A socket sits against the eye as the receiver, a nut and washer stack bears on the bush from the other side, and winding the nut drags the bush out into the socket.

M16 is the largest rod that still passes through the bush sleeves. I kept the thread greased so the nut would not gall under load.

{% include image-gallery.html images="rod-press.jpg, press-rod-bench.jpg" height="500" caption="Figure 2: The puller on a rear lower link, with a zinc-yellow M16 flange nut being wound into the eye and the shell emerging towards the 36 mm receiver socket (left). The same rod after the A-frame chassis bushes, considerably bent, with what is left of a thrust bearing above it (right)." %}

Nearly all of the grief came from what sits on either end of that rod.

The receiver has to be a socket the rod passes straight through, and M16 only clears a 3/4 inch drive square. Every socket I own is 1/2 or 3/8 drive bar one, a 36 mm, so that one socket did most of the job. Wherever it was the wrong size for the eye, the arm canted on the rod and the pull ran off centre.

The pusher is every bit as critical. Anything smaller than the shell punches the rubber and inner sleeve out and leaves the shell sitting in the eye. Two improvised pushers did work. An M20 nut slides over the M16 rod without catching the threads much, though it probably chews them up a little, and the points of its hex are wide enough to bite on the edge of the shell. The other is an M16 flange nut wound straight into the bush. The eye shears the flange down to exactly the bore diameter as it goes in, which makes it a perfect fit. It is far from ideal, because your hands are right beside razor-sharp slivers of steel the whole time you are winding the nut in.

## Problems and Fixes

**Seized shells in the rear lower links.** The press tore the rubber and inner sleeve out and left the outer shell rusted solid in the eye. I drilled out the leftover rubber, sawed through the shell from the inside in eight places with a hacksaw blade and drove it out with a chisel. That was most of a day gone. I had already started pricing replacement arms, and the only standard-height ones were in the UK. Once the eye was clean, the poly bushes pushed in by hand.

{% include image-gallery.html images="lower-link-shell.jpg, lower-link-eye.jpg" height="450" caption="Figure 3: The outer shell after being cut and chiselled out, and the cleaned eye." %}

**Panhard rod bolt fouled a bracket.** With the nut off, the chassis-end bolt ran straight into the radius arm bracket on its way out. Jacking that chassis rail up a touch, front tyres still on the ground, opened just enough room for the bolt head to clear.

**Panhard rod bushes.** Two bushes, and they swallowed hours. Nothing else in the suspension job was slower. I pushed them out with a nut and washer stack on the rod, but the 36 mm socket is too big for these eyes. The eye would not seat square on it, so every time the load came on the arm canted along the rod and lost concentricity. A weld bead on the face of the second eye made it worse again.

**Sleeves would not line up on refit.** With the panhard rod off, the axle had crept sideways. A ratchet strap between axle and chassis hauled it back until both bolts dropped through.

**Wrong thread pitch.** The factory M16 suspension bolts are fine pitch, 1.5 mm. The Class 10 lock nuts I had ordered as their equivalents were coarse, and I only found that out with the panhard rod in my hands. It went back in on new M16 x 100 grade 10.9 coarse bolts I was holding for the radius arms, which the nuts do fit.

**Front dampers spinning.** The nyloc nut on the end of each damper rod just spun the whole damper with it, and I could not hold the damper still enough to turn the nut. I wound a ratchet strap around the damper body and twisted it up tight with vice grips. That locked it until I could get the vice grips onto the flats of the damper rod.

{% include image-gallery.html images="axle-strap.jpg, spring-strap.jpg" height="450" caption="Figure 4: Two jobs for a ratchet strap. Hauling the front axle across to line up the panhard bolts (left). Wound around a front damper and twisted tight with vice grips to stop it spinning with the nut (right)." %}

**Torquing at ride height.** Final torque has to go on with the car back on its wheels, and that leaves next to no room underneath. The panhard rod bolts want 250 Nm. My torque wrench is a metre long with a coarse ratchet, and down there I often could not swing it far enough to catch the next tooth.

{% include image-gallery.html images="torque-ride-height.jpg" height="400" caption="Figure 5: The torque wrench on a rear lower link with the car on its wheels." %}

**A-frame chassis bushes.** After the beating the lower links and the panhard rod handed out, I soaked every remaining bush in my special blend of herbs and spices: diesel, kerosene, penetrating oil and whatever other thin oils and solvents I had saved as waste. It worked a treat on these.

I had always intended to pull them with the high-tensile rod. A cheap bush press kit only came out for its cups, which step up in small sizes and finally gave me a receiver and a pusher that fitted. The snag is that those cups centre on the kit's own locating nut and thrust bearing, both made for its thinner rod, so I had no means of holding them concentric on mine. I ran the kit's rod. It snapped under load and took the thrust bearing race with it. I then bodged the broken locating pieces onto the M16 rod to cradle the cups and drove both bushes out with that. By the end the rod was considerably bent.

{% include image-gallery.html images="cracked-washer.jpg" height="350" caption="Figure 6: Piece of the kit's thrust bearing race, cracked when its rod snapped." %}

**Oil filter didn't match the lookup.** By the VIN this engine takes a paper cartridge in a plastic housing, so that is what I bought. With the sump already drained I went to pull it and found a spin-on can instead. The engine has been rebuilt at some stage and the housing swapped for the spin-on type, which no VIN lookup will ever show. I read the number off the old can and picked up a match locally.

{% include image-gallery.html images="oil-filter.jpg" height="350" caption="Figure 7: The spin-on can that came off in place of the expected cartridge." %}

**Fuel filter priming.** This engine has no lift pump and I don't own the factory priming tool, so I filled the new filter with clean diesel through its outer ports before spinning it on. It started fine.

## Sourcing

Parts came from four suppliers over six orders. I tracked every line in a spreadsheet by job, part number, torque and status.

- My chassis number falls just short of a mid-year change to the front anti-roll bar, so I checked every part number against the chassis number, never just the model year.
- Some Land Rover fasteners I could not buy anywhere in Australia, chiefly the long M16 flange bolts and the shaped washers. I substituted grade 10.9 hex bolts with a hardened structural washer under each head.
- I kept rubber wherever the bush is meant to flex. Polyurethane went in where the part pivots, or where it cost only a few dollars more: the front anti-roll bar mounts, panhard rod, A-frame and rear lower links.
- I bought the A-frame ball joint already pressed into its carrier. It costs more than a bare joint and needs no press at all.
- I ordered the front damper bushes to the exact count and came up eight cup washers short on the second side, which stalled that corner for a few days.
- The fastener supplier's price breaks made 20 of something cheaper than 14 more than once. The leftovers went into later jobs.

## Sleeping Platform

Out came the rear and middle seats, and the tub went back to bare panel. The old seat bracket holes were ringed with white corrosion, which I cleaned back and primed. I modelled the frame in Onshape before buying any timber.

{% include image-gallery.html images="frame-cad.webp" height="400" caption="Figure 8: Onshape model of the frame over the measured tub and wheel-box profile." %}

Two 90 x 45 rails sit on the wheel-box ledges and bolt down through the original seat bracket holes, so I never had to drill the body. Four cross members span between them and film-faced ply goes on top. Every unused hole is blanked with a bolt and a rubber washer.

{% include image-gallery.html images="tub-stripped.jpg, frame.jpg" height="500" caption="Figure 9: Stripped tub with the seat bracket holes treated (left). The frame sitting on the wheel-box ledges (right)." %}

The full-width ply panels I had planned would not go through the doors in one piece. I cut them in half, added a member down the middle to carry the new joint and finished with five panels. The front one is hinged so I can reach the footwell underneath.

{% include image-gallery.html images="ply-fitting.jpg, ply-down.jpg" height="500" caption="Figure 10: Fitting the ply around the body profile, and the deck down." %}

### Insulation

Butyl deadener went onto the roof and wheel arches first, then 6 mm closed-cell foam, then stretch carpet glued over the lot. All four doors got butyl on the outer skins and a foam sheet behind the trim in place of the torn factory liners.

{% include image-gallery.html images="roof-butyl.jpg, arch-butyl.jpg" height="500" caption="Figure 11: Butyl sheets on the roof and over a wheel arch." %}

Laying the foam was the most exasperating job of the lot. It is self-adhesive, and I sprayed contact adhesive onto the metal as well for heat resistance. The instant the two touch, it is stuck for good. The foam tears long before the glue lets go, so a sheet that lands 10 mm out stays 10 mm out. Wherever I tried to lift one, the surface ripped and left a ragged finish, and I packed those patches level with offcuts before carpeting over them.

The method that did work was to cut and dry-fit every piece first, stick only the top edge, then peel the backing downwards a little at a time. I cut the joins slightly oversize, pushed them together and sealed them with foil tape. The foam also runs under the frame rails, so its edges are clamped and cannot lift.

{% include image-gallery.html images="arch-foam.jpg, arch-carpet.jpg" height="450" caption="Figure 12: Foam on a wheel arch with taped joins, and the same arch carpeted." %}

Each deck panel is carpeted on its own. The carpet is stretched from the centre outwards and stapled on the underside, so any panel lifts straight out. Artificial turf lines the load floor underneath.

## Still To Do

## Lessons for Next Time

- Penetrating oil is a godsend. A week of daily soaking and every fastener came off easily. Soak the bushes too, and start early.
- Have a spread of 3/4 inch drive sockets on hand, for receiving and for pushing. An oversize receiver cants the part. An undersize pusher rips the centre out and strands the shell.
- Work out how to hold press cups dead concentric on the high-tensile rod before the first pull.
- Get a torque wrench with a fine-tooth ratchet. A smaller swing between bites is everything when a bolt wants 250 Nm and the car is down at ride height.
- Always check the thread pitch on a bolt off the car before ordering nuts for it.
- Never order small hardware to the exact count. A two-dollar washer can stall a whole corner.
- Measure the door opening before cutting ply to full width.
- Template the foam in cardboard and never peel the whole backing at once.
- Update the parts register the day each job finishes. Mine was out of date within a week.
